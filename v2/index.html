The Stagnant Safety Net

Unemployment insurance adequacy in the District of Columbia, Maryland and Virginia, and the public bodies that set it.

Abstract

Unemployment insurance (UI) is meant to replace part of a laid-off worker's wages while they look for work. This paper measures how far the maximum weekly benefit in DC, Maryland and Virginia goes against housing costs, inflation and wages in 2026. Using U.S. Department of Labor law summaries, BLS wage and price data and HUD Fair Market Rents for the Washington metro area, it finds that the maximum benefit in all three jurisdictions falls short of a two-bedroom Fair Market Rent. In DC and Maryland it also falls short of a one-bedroom. Maryland's $430 maximum has not changed since 2010 and has lost 35% of its purchasing power. DC's maximum replaces 18.6% of the average weekly wage, the lowest share in the region. Virginia raised its maximum twice in 2026, to $478. The paper closes with the bodies that set these values, the bills considered in 2026 and the trade-offs of the main policy options.

Section 1 · Context

Why the maximum benefit matters

Each state, and the District, sets its own UI benefit formula, maximum weekly benefit and taxable wage base within broad federal rules. Most claimants with steady middle-class earnings hit the maximum, so the cap, not the formula, decides what they receive. When a cap stays flat while rents and wages rise, the program replaces a smaller share of lost income each year without any vote to cut it.

The Washington region gives an unusual test. DC, suburban Maryland and Northern Virginia share one labor market and one HUD rent area, yet each jurisdiction sets a different benefit. In 2026 the region also absorbed federal workforce reductions: the federal civilian workforce shrank by about 238,000 people (10.3%) in 2025, according to Pew Research Center's analysis of OPM data. Many of those workers file in these three systems.

3.2 Frozen maximums lose value every year

Maryland's $430 is worth about $278 in today's prices, a 35% loss. Indexed to inflation since 2010 it would be about $664. DC's $444 is worth about $341, a 23% loss since 2020; indexed, it would be about $578. Virginia's maximum stayed at $378 for more than a decade before rising to $430 in January 2026 and $478 in July 2026.

3.4 Part-time work reduces benefits steeply

Claimants are encouraged to take part-time or gig work while they search, but each state deducts earnings above a small disregard. For a claimant at the maximum who earns $250 in a week, the DOL law summaries imply these reductions:

3.5 The financing base has not kept pace with wages

Employers pay state UI tax only on each worker's first $8,000 to $9,000 of wages. That base equals 7.2% of the average annual wage in DC, 10.7% in Maryland and 10.2% in Virginia. The federal base under FUTA has been $7,000 since 1983. A low, fixed base makes the tax fall more heavily on lower-wage payrolls and limits how much a trust fund can grow without raising rates. This paper does not estimate a revenue shortfall; version 1's $601 million estimate has been withdrawn pending a re-estimate from DOL financial data (see Section 6).

Section 4 · Who sets the numbers

The bodies that decide, and when they're on the ballot

Benefit maximums, wage bases and earnings disregards are set by state legislatures and the DC Council, not by Congress. Congress sets the federal framework: the FUTA tax and its $7,000 base, federal standards for state programs, and emergency federal benefits during recessions.

Section 5 · Policy options

Options and their trade-offs

OptionWhat it doesTrade-offs
Index the maximum to wagesSets the cap as a share of the state average weekly wage (Maryland's SB 3 used 40%), so it adjusts every year without new legislation.Raises benefit costs as wages grow; needs matching financing to protect trust-fund solvency.
Index the maximum to pricesAdjusts the cap each year by CPI so its purchasing power holds.Smaller increases than wage indexing; still needs financing.
Raise or index the taxable wage baseTaxes more of each worker's wages, often paired with lower rates.Increases cost for employers with higher-paid staff; can be designed to be revenue-neutral.
Raise the earnings disregardLets claimants keep more of part-time earnings before benefits are cut.Modest cost; may lengthen some claims while increasing work activity during them.
Change dependent allowancesAdds to the benefit for each dependent (Maryland pays $8 per dependent).Targets households with children; adds administrative verification.

Section 6 · Limitations

What this paper does not show

  • All measures use the maximum benefit. Typical benefits are lower, so typical shortfalls are larger.
  • The Washington metro FMR is used for all three jurisdictions. Rents in Baltimore, Richmond and rural areas are lower, so coverage there is higher. FMRs are payment standards, not observed asking rents.
  • Average weekly wages are for one quarter (Q3 2025) and are pulled up by high earners, especially in DC.
  • The partial-earnings figures rely on DOL's summary of each law, not the statutes themselves.
  • Trust-fund adequacy and revenue estimates are out of scope for this version.

Unemployment insurance in the Washington region, October 2026

  1. Maximum weekly benefits: DC $444 (since 2020), Maryland $430 (since 2010), Virginia $478 (since July 5, 2026).

What changed
